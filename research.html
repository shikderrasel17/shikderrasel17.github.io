Research

Explainable machine learning, mostly around education and health decisions. Still early days.

What I'm actually asking

I keep coming back to one question: can a machine learning model be useful to someone who has no reason to trust it? In a well-resourced setting, that question matters less — there's usually a data team standing behind the model, ready to explain it. In the settings I care about, there usually isn't. A university advisor deciding who needs extra support, or a clinic worker looking at a risk score, has to take the model's word for it or ignore it. Most of the time, they ignore it, and I don't blame them.

So the research I work on tries to make that trust a little easier to earn. In practice that means using explainable ML methods — models built so their reasoning can be shown, not just their output — on two problems: predicting which students are likely to drop out, and looking at how e-health decisions get made and supported by data. Both areas share the same constraint: limited data, limited technical staff, and decisions that genuinely affect people's lives.

I didn't come to this from a pure research background. Before I started thinking seriously about a PhD, I was coordinating data and reporting systems for a thirteen-center eye-care network, and I now run sales and executive reporting for a large industrial group. Neither job was research, but both put me in rooms where someone had to explain a number to a person who had every right to ask "why should I believe this." That's the question my research is actually trying to answer, just with more rigor than a Tuesday morning meeting allows.

What I'm working on

Explainable ML for student dropout risk

Trying to build models that flag at-risk students early enough, and explain themselves clearly enough, that an academic advisor with no machine learning background can actually act on the flag instead of just filing it away. I care more about whether the explanation holds up across different groups of students than about squeezing out one more point of accuracy.

Fairness in South Asian e-learning data

A closer look at how dropout and performance models behave once you split the data by gender, region, or income — using e-learning data from South Asia specifically, because most of the fairness research I could find was built on data from richer, more connected education systems that don't look much like the ones I grew up in.

Information systems for e-health decisions

Drawing directly on my time coordinating a Hospital Information System across a multi-center eye-care network — thinking through which ERP and HIS design choices actually speed up good clinical and operational decisions, and where adding an analytics layer helps versus where it just adds noise.

Tools I actually use

Python (Pandas, scikit-learn, TensorFlow, Keras) Explainable ML (SHAP-style interpretation) Statistical testing (Stata, SPSS) Survey and institutional data analysis SQL Power BI / Tableau
