BG

Sales-MIS at an industrial group

Where: Bashundhara Group, piling, ready-mix concrete, and cement businesses, Dhaka. Current job, since January 2026.

What was actually going wrong: Sales targets, achievement tracking, and price approvals lived across SAP and a pile of separate spreadsheets. Every team had its own version of the numbers, which meant meetings often started with arguing about whose number was right before anyone could talk about what to do about it.

What I actually do: I own the SAP side of the sales order process, the transaction codes, the customer master data, the custom reports, plus the sales analytics that feed into a Monthly Chairman's Report that goes to the top of the company. It is less glamorous than it sounds. A lot of it is checking numbers twice before anyone else sees them.

How: Pulling from SAP, building and maintaining Power BI dashboards, and using Excel and Python when I need to reconcile something that does not add up on its own.

What actually changed: There is now one place people go to check target versus achievement numbers instead of five different spreadsheets that never quite agreed with each other. It is not a dramatic transformation. It is fewer arguments in meetings, and reports that go out on time instead of being chased down.

QF

Reporting for a 13-center eye-care network

Where: Quasem Foundation, 3 eye hospitals, 1 maternity hospital, 9 vision centers. May 2025 to January 2026.

What was actually going wrong: Every center tracked its own numbers its own way, so there was no single, trustworthy view of how the whole network was doing. International donors, Seva Foundation, Cure Blindness Project, SightSavers, needed regular reports, and putting those together meant someone manually chasing numbers from thirteen different places every single time.

What I actually did: I was responsible for the ERP and Hospital Information System reporting across all thirteen centers, end to end. That meant building the KPI reports themselves, but also just as much time spent getting different center managers to actually use the same format so the numbers could be compared at all.

How: Power BI, Excel, and SQL for the reporting pipelines, standardized templates for the donor reports, and a fair amount of back and forth on the Vision Center service model, since a lot of the reporting problems traced back to how that model was set up in the first place.

What actually changed: Donor reports went from something assembled by hand every cycle to something that mostly ran itself once the pipeline was set up. I will not claim it was flawless, some centers were slower to adapt than others, but it was a real improvement over the manual version.

GI

Databases for donor-funded programs

Where: Geist International Foundation, Dhaka. Business Development Intern, then Administrative Operations Manager. May 2023 to September 2024.

What was actually going wrong: The organization ran donor-funded programs, including the English Access Micro-scholarship Program with the U.S. Department of State, covering over 500 students, and needed reliable records for student tracking, finance, and program impact that funders could actually check.

What I actually did: Built and maintained the databases behind those programs, mostly in spreadsheets and simple database tools, not anything fancy. This was my first real job out of university, and a lot of it was learning by doing, figuring out what a funder actually needed to see, then building toward that.

What actually changed: The organization had something dependable to point to when a funder asked for numbers, instead of scrambling to pull records together each time. Nothing revolutionary, but it held up under scrutiny, which mattered more than anything else in that context.
